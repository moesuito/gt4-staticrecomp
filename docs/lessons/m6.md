# M6 lesson — reading real R5900 code

Prepared 2026-10-01. BUILD/VERIFY: passed, see the
[M6 evidence](../reverse-engineering/m6-disassembly.md). EXPLAIN: this lesson is
the worked explanation; tutoring review is still pending.

## Objective and motivation

A disassembler turns the game's stored 32-bit instruction words back into
readable instructions. It is the first step of recompilation that touches real
game code: later milestones (control flow, translated C++) all build on this
reading. The goal of this lesson is to explain how one word becomes one
instruction, why branches behave strangely, and what our verification can and
cannot prove.

One failure worth internalizing up front: a disassembler that misreads one
branch can send every later analysis down the wrong path. Because of that, we
never guess. Words outside the implemented subset are reported explicitly as
`unsupported`, and nothing downstream assumes they are ordinary instructions.

## From bytes to an instruction

The CORE contains the game executable as little-endian bytes. Four bytes make
one instruction word. Reading them in order (lowest byte first) gives a
32-bit value; the processor then splits that value into fields:

```text
31        26 25   21 20   16 15   11 10    6 5      0
+-----------+-------+-------+-------+-------+--------+
|  opcode   |  rs   |  rt   |  rd   | shamt | funct  |
+-----------+-------+-------+-------+-------+--------+

I-format (arithmetic with an immediate, loads/stores, branches):
bits 15-0 are a 16-bit immediate (sometimes signed).

J-format (jumps):
bits 25-0 are an index into the current 256 MB region.
```

The `opcode` field selects a family. For `opcode = 0` the `funct` field selects
the exact operation; for `opcode = 1` the `rt` field selects one branch variant.
This is why our unsupported report prints `opcode=0x1c function=0x28` — the
family and the selector, not a guess at the instruction.

### Worked decode: one real branch from the game

Take the word `0x12400011` (it appears at guest address `0x005a3168` in the
pinned CORE). Decode it by hand:

1. `opcode = 0x12400011 >> 26 = 0x04` → `beq` (branch if equal).
2. `rs = (word >> 21) & 0x1f = 18` → register `s2`.
3. `rt = (word >> 16) & 0x1f = 0` → register `zero`.
4. `immediate = word & 0xffff = 0x0011` → 17, in instructions.
5. Branch target = `pc + 4 + 17 * 4` = `0x005a3168 + 4 + 0x44 = 0x005a31b0`.

So the game asks: *if s2 equals zero, continue at 0x005a31b0*. Our
`gt4disasm` prints exactly `beq s2, zero, 0x005a31b0`, and Ghidra independently
prints the same instruction. Two different programs, one shared reading.

## The delay slot: predictable weirdness

On this processor, the instruction immediately after a branch or jump always
executes **before** the transfer happens. It is called the delay slot. In the
example above, the word at `0x005a316c` is `daddu s3, a0, zero` — it runs
*before* the `beq` decision takes effect:

```text
0x005a3168: beq   s2, zero, 0x005a31b0   ; decision made, but...
0x005a316c: daddu s3, a0, zero           ; ...this runs first
0x005a3170: <next instruction>           ; runs only when the branch is not taken
```

This is why the M7 work uses the phrase "delay-slot-aware": any control-flow
walk must remember that every branch and jump owns the following word. Ignoring
delay slots produces listings that look right but describe the wrong order of
execution.

## The explicit-subset policy

Our decoder implements a subset and states it plainly. A word outside the
subset is printed raw with its family, the walker stops there, and reports
count the families:

- Supported: the M5 arithmetic/logic set, ANDI/ORI shifts and immediates, the
  branch/jump family, and — since 2026-10-01 — `slt`, `sltu`, `daddu`, `lh`,
  `sb`, `ld`, `sd`, `beql`, `bnel`, `blez`, `bgtz`, the eight REGIMM branches,
  `jalr` and `syscall`.
- Not yet supported on purpose: COP1 floating point (34 words in our regions)
  and MMI multimedia operations (31 words), plus five single encodings. The
  curriculum schedules COP1/MMI for M15-M17, after control flow and a test
  interpreter exist.

Selection is evidence-driven: an operation is added when real regions contain
it. The ten regions originally had 177 unsupported words; after this expansion,
71 remain, and nine of the ten regions are fully understood.

## What verification showed, and what it did not

Ghidra 12.1.3 decodes the same words independently. We require byte-identical
agreement in mnemonic and operands, with a documented list of assembly
*aliases* expanded on the comparison side: `nop`, `li`, `beqz`, `bnez`, `b`,
and — discovered during this verification — `clear` (`daddu rd,zero,zero`) and
one-operand `jalr` (the default link register is `ra`).

The first verification run reported 9 mismatches. All nine were aliases, not
decoding errors: we chose canonical names, Ghidra chose convenient ones. The
fix was to expand the aliases explicitly in `CompareDisassembly.java` with a
check of the actual encoded fields, never by silencing the difference. The
second run: **417 of 488 words matched, 352 non-NOP, 71 unsupported, 0
mismatches**. This number is recorded in
[docs/inputs/usa-v2.00-disassembly.json](../inputs/usa-v2.00-disassembly.json).

What this proves: our reading of these 488 words equals Ghidra's reading.
What it does not prove: that every decodable word is code, that the listing
matches real execution order, or that the game would run. Agreement between
two disassemblers is strong evidence about *decoding*, not about *behavior*.

## Two mistakes the tests caught (and why that is good)

1. Opcode `0x21` was assumed to be `addi`; the hand decode showed it is `lh`
   (load halfword). `addi` never appeared in the regions and was dropped. The
   hand-selected fixture system caught this before any verification ran.
2. The word `0xffffffff` had been a "not supported" test fixture. After adding
   `sd`, that same word became a *valid* `sd ra, -0x1(ra)`. The fixture was
   replaced, and the incident is recorded: expanding a decoder invalidates
   negative fixtures, so the test suite must be re-checked with every
   expansion.

Both incidents are the process working as intended: tests are expected to
disagree with us, and the disagreement is investigated, never explained away.

## Connection to our implementation

| Piece | File | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decode | `src/ee/decode.cpp` | Splits the word into fields and selects the operation. |
| Format | `src/ee/disassemble.cpp` | Prints canonical assembly, targets and unsupported reports. |
| Frontend | `tools/gt4disasm/main.cpp` | Validates arguments, loads the verified CORE, prints a region. |
| Fixtures | `tests/unit/ee_decode_test.cpp`, `ee_disassemble_test.cpp` | 58 hand-selected supported words and 19 negative encodings. |

The walker in `disassemble_region` is deliberately linear: it reads every word
in a caller-selected range and never follows branches. Modeling what a branch
*does* — basic blocks, successors, delay slots — is exactly the M7 milestone,
and starts from this lesson's two tools: correct decoding and explicit
unsupported stops.

   target by hand. Which fields participate?
2. Where is the delay slot of a branch at `0x0010011c`, and why does it run
   first?
3. Why must the walker stop at an unsupported word instead of skipping it like
   a NOP?
4. The 9 Ghidra mismatches were all aliases. Why is expanding an alias with an
   encoding check fundamentally different from ignoring the mismatch?
5. Two disassemblers agree on 417 words. Name one thing this still does not
   prove.
6. Why did adding `sd` invalidate the `0xffffffff` fixture, and what does that
   imply for every future decoder expansion?
